Batchly

A friction-free companion app for tracking macros in home-cooked mixed dishes.

The problem: dishes like casseroles, chili, and soup are nearly impossible to track accurately unless you divide them into precise, pre-weighed portions — and almost nobody actually does that.

Meet Sarah. Three kids, a slow cooker always going, a weight-loss goal she's serious about. Sunday she makes a pot of chicken and rice casserole — enough for the week. Lose It! makes her declare "6 servings" before she's even tasted it. By Wednesday she's guessing whether this bowl is one serving or one and a half. She's stopped trusting the number, so she's stopped logging the casserole at all.

Batchly fixes both ends of that: she weighs ingredients into the pot as she cooks, no re-taring or math — then weighs her bowl at dinner instead of guessing a serving count. Nothing to declare up front, nothing to remember later.

01 · Discovery Process & Methodology

How the problem was found

Starting broad

The process started with a systematic sweep: what are mundane pain points of daily living that people gripe about online? I surveyed complaints across categories — food/kitchen, public etiquette, tech/digital life, household — looking for friction that was common, emotionally resonant, and underserved by existing tools.

The food/kitchen category surfaced a consistent cluster: people who cook mixed, one-vessel meals described tracking macros for those dishes as disproportionately painful compared to anything else, across Reddit, Facebook meal-prep groups, and fitness communities. That narrowed the question to something specific: why is tracking macros for a pot of chili so much harder than a grilled chicken breast, and what would it take to fix it?

Qualitative research

I posted to Reddit (r/mealprep-style communities) and a Facebook meal-prep group asking about pain points tracking home-cooked meal nutrition — deliberately without mentioning any proposed solution, to avoid leading responses. That choice mattered: I wanted to validate the pain independent of my hypothesis, since confirmation bias is the most common failure mode in customer discovery.

Firsthand competitor testing

I downloaded and tested the two closest competitors myself — Cronometer and MacroFactor — rather than relying on marketing copy or reviews. Details in Section 2.

Two structured interviews

A conversation with Marshall, a MacroFactor user who has lost over 100 pounds tracking his food, confirmed the friction gap from a different angle and sharpened who the product is actually for. A second conversation, with a friend who matches the target user almost exactly — a mom of two who primarily batch-cooks mixed one-pot dishes and is also on a weight-loss journey — surfaced both core pain points unprompted from a single open-ended question. Details in Section 3.

A note on methodology, given the timeline

The standard approach for this kind of discovery is 5–7 structured interviews, asking "tell me about the last time you tried to do X" rather than "what would you do if" — people describe problems as solutions, and the real problem is underneath. Given a Monday-to-Thursday, four-day window, I substituted public-forum discovery (unprompted, unleading) plus two structured interviews for a full interview round. Section 5 outlines what a fuller round would add.
02 · Secondary Research Findings

Competitive landscape and market context

The core question going in: does this gap actually exist in the market, or does an incumbent already solve it well? Secondary research — competitor testing plus public market data — said the gap is real, but it's a workflow gap, not a math gap.

Competitive landscape

ProductWhat it doesWhere it falls short
MyFitnessPal / Lose It! Manual per-ingredient recipe logging; recipes are saved against a declared serving count Heavy manual entry, plus a serving-count mismatch problem: once you declare "this makes 6 servings," accuracy depends on portioning leftovers to match that number, or remembering the per-serving gram weight days later
Cronometer Recipe function records raw and cooked weight Still requires weighing each ingredient individually; no in-app place to record the cooking vessel's weight — pot weight has to be tracked elsewhere
MacroFactor Scoop a portion from a finished "combo" meal, record the weight No guided build workflow — no cumulative weighing, no running total, no raw-to-cooked density calculation

Neither of the two closest competitors delivers the complete loop: tare the pot in-app, add ingredients cumulatively, weigh once after cooking, then just scoop–weigh–log going forward.

Market context

  • The global diet and nutrition apps market is valued at roughly $5.8B (2025), projected to reach ~$10.2B by 2030 at a ~12% CAGR. [source]
  • An estimated 192 million people actively use nutrition/diet tracking apps worldwide. [source]
  • Retention is the industry's structural weak point: health and fitness apps broadly lose the majority of daily users within the first few days of install, and 30-day retention across the category averages in the single digits to low teens. [source]
  • AI-assisted photo and voice logging is visibly reshaping the category — most nutrition app roundups published in 2026 now lead with AI logging over manual entry [source] — a shift that trades accuracy for speed and underserves users who need precise macros.
Pulled from public web research under time pressure, not a single licensed report — sourced above rather than left as unlinked numbers.

What this ruled in and out

The innovation here isn't new math — dividing raw macros by cooked weight already exists in spreadsheets and in Cronometer. What's missing is a friction-free workflow: cumulative weighing without re-taring between ingredients, in-app vessel tracking, and one-tap portion logging. That reframed the product from "a better macro database" to "fewer steps to the same number."

03 · Qualitative Data & How It Was Quantified

What people said, and how it was weighed

Given the timeline, this wasn't a formal survey with a representative sample. Instead, the signal was built by counting how many independent, unprompted sources named the same friction point without being asked about it directly. Six did — across two forums and two live interviews — which is treated here as a directional pattern, not a statistically representative result.

Featured — structured interview, target persona

A structured interview with a friend who matches the target user almost exactly: a mom of two who primarily batch-cooks mixed one-pot dishes — chicken and rice casserole, chicken soup, stews, tuna noodle casserole — and is also on a weight-loss journey. Asked one open-ended question, "What is the hardest part about tracking your food in your particular cooking context?", she named both core pain points unprompted, without being led toward either:

She doesn't want to separate her leftovers into precisely-weighed portions — and she primarily cooks in one pot, weighing as she adds.

Asked how she'd want this to actually work, she said she'd use a "calculator" while cooking, then "copy in the measurements" into Lose It!, her tracker of choice — independently describing the companion-app model this product is built around, unprompted.

Honest limits of this data

This is six sources, not five to seven structured interviews — the difference still matters. Forum posts are self-selected and can't be probed for follow-up the way an interview can, and two conversations is not five to seven. Of the two interviews, one closes the biggest gap in the rest of the data: it's a conversation with someone who is the target persona, not adjacent to it the way Marshall is, and she named both mechanics — plus the companion-app distribution model — unprompted. What this data supports well: the pain is real, named unprompted, and named by the right user profile. What it doesn't yet support: precise frequency (how common is this, really?) or willingness to pay. Section 5 covers what a fuller round would add.

04 · Prototype / Proposed Solution

What was built

Batchly is a single self-contained HTML file — no build step, no account, no API dependency. That's a deliberate scoping choice: with four days on the clock, it kept the build budget pointed at proving the core mechanic, not setting up infrastructure.

The core mechanic

  • Cumulative weighing, no per-ingredient tare. The user enters the scale's current cumulative reading after adding each ingredient; the app tracks the running total in software, independent of the scale display.
  • Re-tare without data loss. If the physical scale gets re-zeroed mid-build, the app stores an offset and keeps computing the running total correctly — the actual fix for the friction point named in discovery.
  • Raw macros are conserved through cooking. Protein/carb/fat mass doesn't change when water evaporates, so cooked totals equal raw totals; dividing by cooked weight gives an accurate density per gram with no separate cooked-nutrition lookup.
  • Portion logging. Weigh a serving, get its macros via weight × density; the dish's remaining weight decrements automatically.

Scope

In scope (v1)
  • Single-vessel dish, cumulative weighing
  • Software-tracked running total & re-tare
  • Raw + cooked macro calculation
  • Portion logging with remaining-weight tracking
  • ~60-item curated ingredient library + custom entry
Out of scope (v1)
  • Multi-component plated meals (e.g. salmon + asparagus)
  • Volume-to-weight conversion (cups/tbsp)
  • Live nutrition database API / AI ingredient matching
  • Blended plate-average problem — documented limitation, not solved
  • Safari / iOS — v1 targets Chrome Desktop and Chrome Android only
Worth naming directly rather than leaving buried in scope docs: the target user is a mom on her phone in the kitchen, and a real share of that audience is on iPhone. Chrome-only in v1 means some of the exact people this is built for can't open it yet — an honest gap, not an oversight, and the first browser-support work I'd pick up next.

How it was built

Rather than building freeform, the prototype followed spec-driven development inside an agile-style workflow — each phase produces a written artifact before code gets touched, and the requirements get broken into epics and stories the way a sprint backlog would.

  • Analyst. Problem statement, target user, and discovery findings written up before any product decisions.
  • PM. Requirements turned into explicit in-scope/out-of-scope calls and a success metric.
  • Architect. Technical design decisions — data model, calculation logic — documented and reasoned through before implementation.
  • Dev. Requirements broken into epics, then stories, each written as "As a [user], I want [X], so that [Y]" with acceptance criteria in given/when/then format, and tracked through backlog → in-progress → review → done like a sprint board.

Each story went through code review before being marked done, and anything flagged but not worth fixing immediately was logged rather than silently dropped — the same discipline a real team applies to technical debt. When new information changed scope mid-build, it went through a short written change proposal instead of getting folded in ad hoc.

User stories the prototype addresses

Epic 1 — Build a Dish

As a home cook adding ingredients to a pot, I want to enter the cumulative scale reading after each ingredient and see a running total of weight and macros, so that I can keep cooking without re-taring between every addition.

As a home cook whose pot is overflowing or who needs to switch vessels, I want to re-tare the scale mid-build with one tap and keep cooking without losing any data, so that I'm never forced to stop tracking just because the scale needs re-zeroing.

Epic 2 — Log Portions from a Batch

As a home cook eating from a batch, I want to select a batch, weigh my portion, and instantly see its calories, protein, carbs, and fat, so that I can track what I'm eating without any manual math.

Built after Epic 1 by design — the density number this depends on had to be right first.

Epic 3 — Save & Reuse Dish Templates

As a home cook who just built a dish, I want it saved as a reusable template so I can find it again later, so that I don't have to re-enter the ingredient list every time I cook the same meal.

Rounds out the loop for repeat cooking — sequenced last since it builds on both epics above.

Full acceptance criteria for each story, plus the data model and scoping decisions behind them, are documented in the underlying project files and available on request.

05 · Retro

What went well, what didn't, what's next

What didn't go well: the accuracy assumption

Raw macros are assumed conserved through cooking (cooked totals = raw totals, only weight changes). That assumption breaks if fat is drained or the dish is stirred/reduced unevenly before portioning — fat-draining changes total fat mass, and uneven reduction can make later portions denser than earlier ones. Flagged as a known v1 assumption: the dish is homogeneous and nothing is added or removed between "cooked weight" and the start of portioning.

That's a real crack in a product whose whole pitch is precision over the AI-photo-logging alternative — worth more than a silent footnote. The v1-appropriate mitigation isn't a math fix, it's a visibility one: a one-line reminder at the "mark as cooked" step ("Density assumes nothing is drained, added, or removed before portioning") turns a silent accuracy failure into a stated assumption the user can judge for themselves. It doesn't solve the underlying problem, but it stops the product from quietly claiming precision it can't back up in that specific case.

Parked ideas, not yet validated

  • Shareable dish templates — "Mom's Turkey Chili, 247 cal/100g" as a reusable, shareable template; a possible content/acquisition channel.
  • "What's in the fridge" leftover tracking — surfacing remaining cooked weight as a daily-use inventory view, addressing retention.
  • Smart scale integration — Bluetooth pairing to remove the last manual step (reading and typing the weight).
  • Recipe import from the web — pull an ingredient list straight from a recipe URL instead of building it entry by entry, so setting up a template is closer to one paste than a manual build.

Expansion vision

  1. Companion integration — API connection to Lose It! or MyFitnessPal; push a built dish's nutrition entry to the primary tracker with one tap.
  2. Feature expansion — volume-to-weight helpers, multi-component plated meals, richer ingredient database via API or community contribution.
  3. Strategic outcome — if the companion model shows adoption, a natural acquisition target for an established tracker; if users signal demand for standalone use, expand into a full tracker built around the home-cook workflow.

What I'd do differently with more time

With more than four days: 5–7 structured interviews using "tell me about the last time you tried to track a mixed dish" rather than hypothetical framing, real theme-frequency counting instead of a five-source pattern match, and a check on willingness to pay before assuming the companion-app model is the right monetization path.
